Product Thinking · Lesson 1 of the IDG pathway

Solutions in disguise

How to hear “we need X” and find the real problem underneath it.

Your win for this lesson When someone says “we need an app”, you'll recognise it as a solution, and you'll know the three questions that lead back to the problem.

Why this matters

The Institute of Digital Government (IDG) begins its whole product-thinking course with one claim:

Great digital products start with problems, not solutions. Projects can struggle even when execution is strong, especially when they are solving the wrong problem. IDG, Module 1: Understanding the Problem

The risk is not a badly built product. It is a well-built product that nobody needed. In IDG's words, we can end up “building the wrong thing entirely: on time, on budget, and completely unhelpful.”[1]

The common trap

Requests often sound like problems when they are really answers someone has already picked. IDG gives four government examples:[1]

What we hearThe real issue
“We need more AI.”Officers struggle to make decisions from large, unwieldy datasets.
“We need a superapp.”Citizens can't access services efficiently across platforms.
“We don't have a CRM.”Cumbersome processes are causing long wait times for citizens.
“Our product is end of life.”Citizens and officers will lose access to critical services.

Look at the difference. Each left-hand statement names a thing to build. Each right-hand statement names a person and what is going wrong for them. You can argue about the best fix for the right-hand side. The left-hand side has skipped that argument entirely.

Solution in disguise

Names a technology, product or feature. Nobody is mentioned, and nothing is said about what's broken.

Problem

Names who is affected, what they're trying to do, and what's getting in their way.

The three questions

IDG's first principle is focus on the problem, not the solution. It comes with three questions you can ask about any request:[1]

  1. Who are our users?
  2. What are they trying to do?
  3. What's getting in their way?

If a request can't answer all three, it isn't ready to be built.

A small but important point. Often you can't answer the three questions from your desk. When you reframe a request, what you write down is a guess about the problem, and you have to check it with real users. Later lessons cover how: the Five Whys, and gathering evidence with the 4Cs.

A side note from the same module: Grab began with “make taxis safer, more reliable, easier to book”, and Amazon began with books alone. Neither started with a superapp. “The foundation wasn't technology, it was a precise understanding of one problem, solved exceptionally well.”[1]

Practice 1 · Problem or solution?

Sort each statement. Try to decide before you look at the options.

“We need a chatbot for the housing portal.”

“Elderly residents miss polyclinic appointments because reminder letters arrive after the appointment date.”

“Our case management system needs to move to the cloud.”

“Officers can't tell which of 2,000 daily feedback emails are urgent, so urgent cases wait five days.”

“Let's build a real-time dashboard for senior management.”

Practice 2 · Pick the best reframing

A library manager says: “We need a mobile app for book renewals.”

Which statement best describes the underlying problem?

Practice 3 · Your turn (free recall)

A town council director says: “We need a WhatsApp bot for residents.”

Without scrolling up, write the three questions you'd ask, and draft one possible problem statement the bot might be meant to solve.

Model answer. The questions: Who are our users? What are they trying to do? What's getting in their way?

One possible problem: “Residents reporting faults (broken lights, lifts) can only call during office hours, so issues reported in the evening wait until the next working day.”

Your version will probably differ, and that's the point. Before you write it, you don't know what the real problem is. That tells you to go and talk to residents and council officers. Check that your answer has a who, a what and an obstacle, and that it doesn't mention WhatsApp.

What's next

IDG's second principle is chase outcomes, not outputs. Their example: “A website with five buttons is an output. More citizens completing transactions is an outcome.” The next lesson turns that into a skill. Before you open it, try to explain the difference from memory.
